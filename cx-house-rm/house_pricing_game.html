The loop you kept running
  • Guess a value for ŵ.
  • Predict the price: ŷ = ŵ · x.
  • Compare with what the house really sold for: the miss l = y − ŷ.
  • Update ŵ using the miss, and go again.

The only step you improvised was the last one. Let's pin it down.

Which way, and how far?

Two ways to turn the shout into a correction. You probably used the second one without noticing.

Fixed step: the first instinct

if l < 0: ŵ ← ŵ − α
else:    ŵ ← ŵ + α

Only the sign of the miss is used. Every step is the same size: painfully slow when you are far off, jittery once you are close.

Proportional step: what you actually did

ŵ ← ŵ + α · l

Use the size of the miss too. Shouted "too low by 116,889"? Big correction. "Too high by 111"? A nudge. One rule handles both.

The fixed-step algorithm.

Here is the whole thing, written as a program.

given   x, y
guess   ŵ
do
    ŷ = ŵ · x
    l = y − ŷ
    ŵ ← ŵ + α · l
until   |l| < ε

  • ŵ: your current guess for the price per m².
  • l: the signed miss, and its direction. Positive means raise the price.
  • α: the step size. You choose it, and it scales a miss in CHF into a change in CHF/m².
  • ε: the tolerance. Stop once the owner is convinced.

Why weight by area? A miss on a 234 m² house says more about the price per square metre than the same miss on a 62 m² house. The base price is added to every house alike, so for b every miss counts the same.

Reading the shout.

The market shouts l = +8,789 for Rosenweg 12. What does that tell you?

l = y − ŷ > 0 means the real price y sits above your prediction ŷ: you under-priced the house by 8,789. The update ŵ ← ŵ + α · l adds a positive amount, so the next prediction is higher. The sign of the miss is the direction of the correction.

Choosing the step size.

You pick a big step size, α = 0.05, for Rosenweg 12 (x = 234). What happens round after round?

A nudge of α · l in w moves the prediction by α · l · x = 11.7 · l. You do not just close the gap, you fly almost twelve gaps past it and land further away on the other side. Whenever α · x > 2 the loop diverges. The right α depends on the scale of x, which is why it looks so tiny here (around 0.001–0.004).

Two knobs, two averages.

In the street loop, why does b listen to the plain average miss while w listens to the area-weighted one?

The plain average only says whether the street is priced too high or too low overall. It cannot tell "too steep" from "too high": small houses under-priced and big houses over-priced can average out to zero with a tilted line. Weighting each miss by the area breaks that tie, because the big houses pull harder on the slope. That is why w needs the weighted average, and why the line ends up through the whole street once both averages are quiet.
